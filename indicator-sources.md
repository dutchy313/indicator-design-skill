# Standard indicator banks and institutional guidance

Harvest from these before writing anything bespoke. Adopting a standard indicator brings ready-made metadata, comparability across programmes, and — critically — a defensible answer when a donor or auditor asks where the definition came from.

Verify current editions online where possible; guidance is revised on multi-year cycles and the notes below reflect the substance rather than a live version number.

## 1. Cross-cutting global frameworks

**SDG Global Indicator Framework (IAEG-SDGs / UN Statistics Division).** 230+ indicators across 17 goals, each with a published metadata sheet giving definition, computation method, data sources, disaggregation and limitations. Indicators carry a tier classification reflecting methodological maturity and data availability. The metadata repository is the single best model for what an indicator reference sheet should contain. Align to SDG indicators wherever a programme claims SDG contribution — it makes national reporting and VNR linkage possible.

**OECD-DAC.** The *Glossary of Key Terms in Evaluation and Results Based Management* is the shared vocabulary of the sector — use its definitions of indicator, output, outcome, impact, baseline and results chain to avoid terminological drift between partners. The DAC evaluation criteria (relevance, coherence, effectiveness, efficiency, impact, sustainability) should shape which questions the indicator set must be able to answer. DAC policy markers (gender equality, environment, climate mitigation/adaptation, disability, biodiversity) provide standardised tagging.

**IATI Standard.** Where results are published to IATI, the results element expects indicator, baseline, target and actual with periods — design the framework so it can be serialised into that structure without rework.

## 2. Multilateral development banks

**World Bank.**
- *Ten Steps to a Results-Based Monitoring and Evaluation System* (Kusek & Rist) — the foundational text for building government M&E systems; source of the CREAM criteria in mainstream use.
- Corporate Results Indicators (CRIs) / standardised sector indicators — mandatory core indicators for IBRD/IDA operations, designed for portfolio aggregation. Check the sector list before drafting; using a CRI verbatim saves negotiation.
- Results framework guidance in project appraisal documentation: PDO-level indicators (few, directly tied to the project development objective) versus intermediate results indicators.
- IEG guidance on results frameworks, and *Designing a Results Framework for Achieving Results*.
- Independent Evaluation Group reviews are a rich source of what fails at closure — worth mining for the failure modes in a given sector.

**African Development Bank** — Results Measurement Framework, four-level structure. **Asian Development Bank** — Development Effectiveness Review indicators. **Islamic Development Bank** — Results Measurement Framework. All three follow a similar corporate scorecard logic and are useful when the programme must roll up to a bank-wide result.

**Global Fund.** The *Monitoring and Evaluation Toolkit* (HIV, TB, malaria and health systems strengthening) is unusually detailed on indicator definitions, data quality and routine-system strengthening. Its modular framework and performance framework structure are worth studying even outside health.

## 3. UN system

**UNICEF.** Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS) supply internationally standardised, tested indicator definitions and questionnaire modules across child health, nutrition, WASH, education, child protection and social protection — including the full computation syntax. Where a MICS indicator exists, use it: the instrument, the definition and the national comparison points come together. UNICEF results-based management guidance and the Strategic Plan results framework define the agency's own hierarchy; country programme documents must align to it.

**UNDP.** *Handbook on Planning, Monitoring and Evaluating for Development Results* — accessible treatment of results chains, indicator selection and evaluation planning; still one of the best single introductions for teams new to RBM. UNDP also publishes standard indicator sets by signature solution.

**WHO.** *Global Reference List of 100 Core Health Indicators* — the reference point for health indicator harmonisation, with definitions, numerators, denominators and preferred data sources. Also foundational work on health information system strengthening and routine data quality (DQR).

**UNAIDS/WHO.** *Indicator Standards: Operational Guidelines for Selecting Indicators for the HIV Response* — the clearest published articulation anywhere of formal indicator appraisal criteria (need, definitional clarity, measurability, validity, reliability, usefulness, feasibility) and a genuinely transferable appraisal process for any sector.

**UN Women** — gender equality indicator guidance and the minimum set of gender indicators (with UNSD). **FAO** — food security and agriculture indicators, including FIES. **ILO** — decent work indicators. **UNESCO Institute for Statistics** — education indicator definitions and the SDG 4 framework. **UNEG** — Norms and Standards for evaluation, which constrain how indicator evidence is later used.

## 4. European Union

**EU Results Framework** — three-level structure (development progress, EU-funded outcomes/outputs, organisational performance) with a standard set of indicators that EU-funded actions are expected to draw from. Where an action is EU-funded, mapping to the EURF indicator list is generally expected, not optional.

**Logical Framework Approach.** EU project cycle management guidance codified the logframe matrix (intervention logic, indicators, sources of verification, assumptions) that most donors still use. The *assumptions* column is the most neglected and most valuable part — it is where the theory of change's fragility is recorded.

**Better Regulation Toolbox** — source of the RACER criteria and of solid practical guidance on monitoring arrangements for policy interventions.

**DG REGIO / cohesion policy monitoring and evaluation guidance** — strong treatment of result indicators versus output indicators, and of the distinction between programme-specific and common indicators. Useful conceptual discipline even outside the EU regional context.

## 5. Bilateral donors

**USAID.** ADS Chapter 201 governs performance monitoring; the *Performance Indicator Reference Sheet (PIRS)* is the most widely copied metadata template in the sector. Standard Foreign Assistance ("F") indicators provide a large, well-documented bank. The Data Quality Assessment framework — validity, integrity, precision, reliability, timeliness — is the de facto standard for indicator data quality, reused far beyond USAID.

**FCDO (formerly DFID).** Smart Rules and results guidance; the logframe format with impact/outcome/output levels and milestone-by-year targets is widely required. FCDO's work on measuring hard-to-measure results (empowerment, governance, resilience) is worth consulting for outcome areas that resist counting.

**GIZ / BMZ** — results-based monitoring guidance, strong on capacity development indicators. **SIDA**, **Global Affairs Canada**, **DFAT**, **Norad** all publish results guidance with broadly convergent logic; the differences are vocabulary, not substance.

## 6. Sector-specific banks

- **Health:** WHO 100 Core Indicators; Global Fund M&E Toolkit; Countdown to 2030; DHIS2 standard data dictionaries; national HMIS indicator manuals (always check these — programme indicators should mirror the national system where possible).
- **Education:** UIS/SDG 4 indicators; EGRA/EGMA instruments; Global Partnership for Education results framework; national EMIS indicator sets.
- **WASH:** WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme service ladders — the authoritative definitions of basic/limited/safely managed water, sanitation and hygiene. Do not invent WASH access categories; the JMP ladders exist.
- **Nutrition:** WHO/UNICEF infant and young child feeding indicators; SMART survey methodology for anthropometry; Scaling Up Nutrition common results framework.
- **Agriculture and food security:** FAO indicators; Food Insecurity Experience Scale; IPC classification; Feed the Future indicator handbook.
- **Social protection:** ISPA tools; World Bank ASPIRE indicators.
- **Governance and PFM:** PEFA framework (public financial management, with defined scoring); Worldwide Governance Indicators; Open Budget Index; TI Corruption Perceptions Index (for context, not attribution).
- **Climate and environment:** Green Climate Fund results management framework; Adaptation Fund core indicators; GEF core indicators; joint MDB climate finance tracking methodology.
- **Energy and infrastructure:** SE4All / Multi-Tier Framework for energy access — again, established tiers exist; do not invent access categories.

## 7. Humanitarian

**Humanitarian Indicator Registry (IASC / Global Clusters)** — standard indicators by cluster with definitions. **Sphere Handbook** — minimum standards with associated key indicators. **Core Humanitarian Standard** — nine commitments with performance indicators for accountability to affected populations. **INFORM Index** for risk context. Humanitarian settings demand faster cadence, lower measurement burden and stronger safety screening on disaggregation than development programming.

## 8. Cross-cutting dimensions

**Gender.** UNSD/UN Women Minimum Set of Gender Indicators; DAC gender equality policy marker; gender-transformative measurement approaches (e.g. WEAI — Women's Empowerment in Agriculture Index — and its abbreviated forms) where agency and empowerment must be measured rather than assumed.

**Disability.** Washington Group Short Set of Questions on Disability — the international standard for disability disaggregation in censuses and surveys; the Child Functioning Module (WG/UNICEF) for children. Use functional-difficulty questions, never a single self-identification item, and never a programme-specific ad hoc definition.

**Environment and climate.** GEF and GCF core indicator sets; MDB joint methodologies for climate finance tracking; the DAC Rio markers.

**Localisation and participation.** Grand Bargain localisation indicators; participatory approaches to indicator definition (SPICED). Where the programme's value proposition is voice or accountability, involve intended participants in defining what improvement looks like — otherwise the indicator measures the funder's theory, not the participants' reality.

## 9. Foundational methodological texts

- Kusek & Rist, *Ten Steps to a Results-Based Monitoring and Evaluation System* (World Bank) — system building.
- Morra Imas & Rist, *The Road to Results* (World Bank) — design and conduct of development evaluations; strong chapter on indicator selection.
- Gertler et al., *Impact Evaluation in Practice* (World Bank) — where indicators meet causal identification; essential when targets imply attribution claims.
- Roche, *Impact Assessment for Development Agencies* (Oxfam) — origin of SPICED; the counterweight to over-quantification.
- Mayne on contribution analysis — how to make defensible claims at outcome and impact level without an experimental design.
- Davies & Dart, *Most Significant Change Technique* — for programmes where the important changes cannot be specified in advance.
- Outcome Mapping (IDRC) and Outcome Harvesting (Wilson-Grau) — behaviour-change measurement for influence, advocacy and systems-change programming where a fixed indicator set is inappropriate.
