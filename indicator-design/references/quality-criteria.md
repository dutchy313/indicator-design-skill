# Indicator quality criteria, appraisal rubric and data quality standards

## 1. The four criteria frameworks

Pick one as the primary lens, state which, and use the others to fill its blind spots. Applying all four to every indicator produces a tick-box exercise and no insight.

### SMART
| Criterion | Question | Blind spot |
|---|---|---|
| Specific | Is the population, place and characteristic unambiguous? | |
| Measurable | Can it be observed and quantified with available methods? | |
| Achievable / Attainable | Is the associated target realistic given resources and timeframe? | |
| Relevant | Does it measure the result, not a proxy of convenience? | |
| Time-bound | Is there a defined period and reporting point? | |

SMART is really a *target* test wearing an indicator's clothes — "achievable" and "time-bound" describe targets, not variables. Use it, but do not stop there: it says nothing about cost or about whether anyone will accept the number.

### CREAM (Schiavo-Campo; used in Kusek & Rist, World Bank)
| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Clear | Precise and unambiguous — would two analysts compute the same value? |
| Relevant | Appropriate to the result being measured |
| Economic | Available at reasonable cost — the criterion SMART omits |
| Adequate | Provides a sufficient basis to assess performance, alone or with others |
| Monitorable | Amenable to independent validation |

Best framework for government systems strengthening and public-sector performance, because *Economic* and *Monitorable* force the sustainability and verifiability questions early.

### RACER (European Commission, Better Regulation)
| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Relevant | Closely linked to the objective |
| Accepted | Do those who must produce and act on it agree it is legitimate? |
| Credible | Unambiguous and easy for non-experts to interpret |
| Easy to monitor | Low collection burden |
| Robust | Resistant to manipulation |

*Accepted* and *Robust* are the two criteria no other framework names, and they are the two that most often determine whether an indicator survives implementation. Use RACER for policy reform, regulatory and institutional programmes.

### SPICED (Roche / Oxfam)
| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Subjective | Does it capture the informed judgement of those closest to the change? |
| Participatory | Were intended participants involved in defining it? |
| Interpreted | Is its meaning explained to those who did not develop it? |
| Cross-checked | Is it triangulated against other sources and methods? |
| Empowering | Does the act of measuring build participants' capacity to reflect and act? |
| Diverse and disaggregated | Does it capture different groups' experiences separately? |

Use in parallel with SMART/CREAM for empowerment, rights, social accountability, gender-transformative and community-driven programming. SMART applied alone to such programmes systematically deletes the outcomes that matter.

## 2. Appraisal rubric

Score each candidate indicator. Anything scoring 0 on **Necessity**, **Definitional clarity** or **Feasibility** is rejected regardless of other scores — these are gates, not weights.

| # | Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Necessity** — a named user makes a named decision with it | No identified use | Reporting requirement only | Drives a specific management or policy decision |
| 2 | **Definitional clarity** — numerator, denominator, inclusion rules | Undefined construct | Partially defined | Fully specified; two analysts would agree |
| 3 | **Validity** — measures the intended result, not an adjacent one | Measures effort or something else | Reasonable proxy, unjustified | Direct measure, or proxy with documented justification |
| 4 | **Reliability** — consistent across time, place and enumerator | Highly method-dependent | Some variability | Stable, standardised instrument |
| 5 | **Sensitivity** — will move within the programme period if the result occurs | Will not move in the timeframe | May move late | Responsive within reporting cycles |
| 6 | **Feasibility and cost** — collectable within MEL budget | Requires unavailable data | Costly but possible | Uses existing/routine systems |
| 7 | **Robustness to gaming** | Trivially gamed | Gameable with effort | Quality qualifier or counter-indicator built in |
| 8 | **Disaggregability** | Cannot be disaggregated | Partially | Supports required LNOB dimensions |
| 9 | **Comparability** | Bespoke, incomparable | Loosely aligned | Matches a recognised standard definition |
| 10 | **Ethics and safety** | Collection risks harm; no protection assessment | Risks identified, unmitigated | Risks assessed and mitigated; consent and data protection defined |

A practical threshold: reject below 12/20, revise 12–15, accept 16+. Publish the scores alongside the framework — it converts indicator selection from a preference argument into a documented decision.

## 3. Data quality standards for verification

The **DQA criteria** (USAID ADS 201, widely reused across the sector):

- **Validity** — the data clearly and adequately represent the intended result.
- **Integrity** — mechanisms exist to prevent unauthorised or unintentional alteration; transcription and manipulation risks are controlled.
- **Precision** — the margin of error is acceptable for the management decisions the data supports. Note that greater precision costs more; specify what precision is *needed*, not the maximum obtainable.
- **Reliability** — consistent collection methods and definitions over time, so trends reflect real change rather than method change.
- **Timeliness** — data are current enough and frequent enough to inform the decision they exist to serve.

WHO's data quality review framework adds a useful routine-systems lens: completeness and timeliness of reporting, internal consistency, external consistency with survey estimates, and consistency of population denominators — the last being an underrated source of error in coverage indicators.

Plan the verification method per indicator at design stage: source document review, recount/re-measurement of a sample, cross-system comparison, third-party monitoring, or community verification.

## 4. Reviewing an existing framework

Work in this order; earlier problems make later ones moot.

1. **Logic before measurement.** Is the results chain coherent? Are outputs genuinely the products of activities, and outcomes genuinely the uptake of outputs? A well-measured incoherent theory of change is still worthless.
2. **Level misclassification.** Count how many "outcome" indicators are actually output counts. This is the most common and most consequential defect.
3. **Definitional audit.** For every rate, is the denominator stated? For every count, is the counting unit and inclusion rule stated? For every "reached", "supported", "trained", "strengthened" — is the threshold defined?
4. **Baseline and target integrity.** Are baselines sourced and dated? Are targets derived or invented? Is cumulative versus periodic stated?
5. **Feasibility.** For each indicator, does the named data source actually exist and does the programme have access to it?
6. **Balance and burden.** Total count; ratio of bespoke to routine data; presence of any indicator capable of showing failure.
7. **Ethics.** Any sensitive attribute collected without a protection rationale.
8. **Use.** Ask what decisions were changed by last cycle's data. If none, the framework's problem is not technical.

Deliver findings ranked by severity with rewritten indicators, not just critique. A review that identifies twenty problems and solves none is a burden on the team, not a service to it.
